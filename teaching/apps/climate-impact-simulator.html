🌍 Climate Impact Simulator Interactive App

Explore how climate, land use, and policy choices interact to shape environmental resilience. 🌱

💡 What is the Climate Impact Simulator?

The Climate Impact Simulator is an interactive Shiny app designed as a teaching and learning tool for:

  • Climate science & geography
  • Environmental sustainability & policy
  • Systems thinking and scenario analysis

Learners can choose a city (or custom coordinates), then explore how:

  • Historical climate patterns (temperature & precipitation),
  • Environmental pressures (deforestation, emissions, water use, etc.), and
  • Policy levers (renewable energy, public transport, reforestation, agriculture)

combine to influence an overall Climate Resilience Index from 0–10.

🌟 App Features

Feature Description
🗺️ Interactive map Select world cities or custom coordinates and see their resilience score on a map
📈 Walter–Lieth climate diagram Visualize monthly temperature and precipitation with dry and frost months highlighted
🌡️ Climate Resilience Index A composite score (0–10) combining forest, air, urban, energy, water, and agriculture components
🎚️ Environmental factor sliders Adjust deforestation, urbanization, renewables, emissions, water use, etc., and watch the index respond in real time
🔄 Scenario presets One-click Current, Optimistic, and Pessimistic scenarios to compare futures
📊 Component & sensitivity plots Radar and bar plots show which factors contribute most to resilience or risk
📥 Report export Generate a summary report (HTML) for assignments, lab notes, or reflection tasks

📊 Data and Approximations

The app is built for conceptual exploration, not for official decision‑making. It uses realistic but simplified data sources:

1. Climate data (temperature & precipitation)

  • For a small set of major cities (e.g. Berlin, London, Paris, New York, Mexico City, São Paulo), the app uses approximate monthly climate normals based on typical climatology (mean monthly temperature and precipitation in the right ballpark for 20th–21st‑century averages).
  • For other locations, the app uses generic climate patterns by latitude band, for example:
    • Low latitudes: warm, wet, weak seasonality (tropical).
    • Mid latitudes: moderate temperatures with clear seasons (temperate/continental).
    • High latitudes: cold, strong seasonal cycle (subarctic).
  • A Walter–Lieth climate diagram is calculated from:
    • Monthly mean temperature (Tmean)
    • Approximate Tmin and Tmax
    • Monthly precipitation (Precip)
  • Dry months (P < 2 × Tmean) and frost months (Tmin < 0 °C) are flagged to support teaching about climate classification and seasonal water balance.

Important:
Climate values are approximate and synthetic, calibrated to be realistic but not taken from a specific gridded dataset or live API. They are suitable for teaching patterns and concepts, not for local planning.

2. Environmental indicators (deforestation, AQI, etc.)

For each selected city/region, the app provides environmental indicators such as:

  • Deforestation rate (%/year)
  • Urbanization rate (%/year)
  • Renewable energy share (%)
  • CO₂ emissions change (%)
  • Sustainable agriculture share (%)
  • Water use change (%)
  • Public transport usage (%)
  • Reforested area (%)
  • Air Quality Index (AQI, 0–500)
  • Fire risk index

These values are:

  • Plausible approximations informed by:
    • Typical values for country income level and region,
    • Known qualitative patterns (e.g. higher AQI in megacities with heavy traffic; higher renewables in some European countries).
  • Not directly sourced from official datasets (e.g. not pulled from WHO, Copernicus, or national statistics in real time).
  • Intended for scenario exploration and comparative teaching, not for quantitative assessment of a specific city.

Each environmental variable can be adjusted manually, and the starting (“Current”) values for well‑known cities are chosen to be reasonable but simplified.

🧮 How the Climate Resilience Index Works (Conceptually)

The app combines several components into a single 0–10 index:

  • Forest & land use (deforestation, reforestation, fire risk)
  • Air quality & emissions (CO₂ change, AQI, renewables)
  • Urban planning & transport (urbanization, public transport)
  • Energy systems (renewables, air quality)
  • Water & agriculture (water use, sustainable agriculture, deforestation)

Each component is:

  1. Scaled to a 0–10 sub‑score, where higher is better (more resilient/sustainable).
  2. Combined with weights into an overall index.

The exact formulas are visible in the code, and you can use them in class to discuss:

  • Why some factors are weighted more heavily (e.g. forests vs. transport),
  • How index construction always involves value judgments and trade‑offs,
  • How different weighting schemes could change rankings.

🎓 Using the App for Teaching & Learning

This app is ideal for:

1. Climate & geography labs

  • Explore how climate types (tropical, temperate, subarctic) look in a Walter–Lieth diagram.
  • Identify dry months and discuss water stress.
  • Compare cities at different latitudes and on different continents.

2. Environmental science & sustainability courses

  • Show how deforestation or higher emissions reduce the resilience index.
  • Demonstrate benefits of increasing renewable energy or public transport.
  • Discuss AQI categories (good, moderate, unhealthy) and their health/environment impacts.

3. Scenario analysis & systems thinking

  • Start with Current conditions for a city.
  • Switch to Optimistic and Pessimistic presets:
    • How do the radar plot and component bars change?
    • Which levers (renewables, reforestation, transport) are most powerful?
  • Assign students to:
    • Design a policy package that raises the score from, say, 5.2 to ≥7.5.
    • Compare trade‑offs (e.g. rapid urbanization vs. investment in public transit).

4. Assignments and reflective reports

  • The app’s report export can be used in:
    • Lab exercises (“Generate a report for Berlin and briefly interpret each component.”)
    • Group projects (“Compare three cities on different continents under optimistic scenarios.”)
  • Students can attach the generated report as an appendix and focus on interpretation and policy discussion.

🚦 What this tool is and is not

✅ Good for:

  • Building intuition about:
    • Climate patterns and diagrams
    • How multiple environmental indicators interact
    • Effects of policy choices and scenarios on a composite index
  • Supporting discussion, debate, and critical thinking in class.

❌ Not for:

  • Official carbon accounting or environmental reporting
  • Detailed risk assessment at city scale
  • Real‑time monitoring of climate or air quality

You should make this clear to students:
Values are approximate and illustrative, not an authoritative database.
